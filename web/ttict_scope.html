·Purpose and How to Use This Document

Why this exists separately from the parliamentary submission, and how the two documents relate.

The parliamentary submission argues the case for the programme and states defensible three-year cost ranges per sector. It deliberately does not carry staffing tables, named equipment vendors, or man-hour detail — that level of granularity does not belong in front of Parliament, and including it would bury the policy argument under procurement mechanics. This document is where that mechanics lives: for each of the seven sectors and both structural pillars, a staffing table with real Trinidad and Tobago wage data and statutory on-costs, a named-vendor equipment table where physical or licensed equipment is involved, and a reconciliation showing how the bottom-up build relates to the top-level Phase 1–3 figures already defended in the parliamentary document.

Every figure here is an AACE International Class 4–5 estimate (order-of-magnitude to preliminary, ±30–50% accuracy) — built from real market rates, real Trinidad and Tobago statutory data, and named vendor pricing pages where public pricing exists, but not yet vendor-quoted. Reaching Class 2–3 (±10–20%, the precision an actual procurement requires) is the job of the RFP schedule in Section 10: this document tells a procurement officer what to ask for and roughly what to expect to pay, not what a vendor will actually quote.

Statutory on-cost standard, applied throughout
Every salary figure in this document carries the employer National Insurance (NIS) contribution: 16.2% combined (employer share ≈10.8%) through December 2026, rising to 19.2% combined (employer share ≈12.8%) from January 2027, calculated on insurable earnings capped at TT$13,600/month. The Health Surcharge (employee-side deduction, max TT$8.25/week) is never added as an employer cost. The Green Fund Levy (0.3% of gross revenue) is a business tax applied once a sector authority or enterprise is revenue-generating, tracked separately from payroll. Where a role's cost spans the January 2027 rate change, both years are shown.
Part I

Structural Pillars — Bottom-Up Build

Executive compensation benchmark — real comparables, not generic professional averages
Director-tier compensation throughout this document is benchmarked against actual, publicly reported Trinidad and Tobago state-enterprise executive pay, not the national average professional salary used for working-level roles. Reported monthly figures for comparable state enterprises: NGC President ≈TT$150,000; Trinidad Petroleum Holdings (successor to Petrotrin) President ≈TT$180,000, Vice-President ≈TT$120,000; TSTT CEO package >TT$200,000; UDeCOTT CEO ≈TT$65,000; NIPDEC General Manager ≈TT$58,250; NIDCO President ≈TT$50,000 basic plus housing, entertainment, and travel allowances. This document applies a two-tier structure: Tier A (national-programme-scale directors overseeing a single-year capital flow in the hundreds of millions to billions of TT dollars — the T&T Yozma Executive Director, the TT Stack Programme Lead, the Sovereign AI Compute Utility negotiation lead) is benchmarked to the NGC/Petrotrin range, TT$120,000–180,000/month; Tier B (sector authority directors overseeing a three-year programme in the tens to low hundreds of millions) is benchmarked to the UDeCOTT/NIPDEC/NIDCO range, TT$55,000–90,000/month. A director paid below what Trinidad and Tobago already pays the head of NIDCO to oversee road and infrastructure projects is not a credible candidate to run a national AI-industry authority, and this document corrects that error explicitly rather than leaving it to be caught at the procurement stage.

2aPillar Two-B — Sovereign AI Compute Export Utility: Power and Generation

The single largest capital line in the whole programme, scoped bottom-up against Trinidad and Tobago's real national generation fleet — and a materially cheaper path exists than pure new-build.

2a.1 The Real Scale Problem, Stated in National Terms

Trinidad and Tobago's total installed national generation capacity is approximately 2,365MW: PowerGen's three plants (Point Lisas 838MW, Port-of-Spain 270MW, Penal 236MW) totalling 1,344MW; TGU's 720MW combined-cycle plant, which alone supplies roughly half the country's electricity; Trinity Power's 225MW; and Tobago's Cove and Scarborough plants adding a further 76MW. The Hummingbird AI Holdings (150–500MW) and Ernst & Young (300MW) MOUs, combined, total 450–800MW — 19% to 34% of the entire country's current generation capacity, for two data-centre projects. TGU itself, the largest single plant in the national fleet, is smaller than the high end of this combined figure. This cannot be served from existing shared grid capacity without materially degrading power for the rest of the country, which already shows real reliability strain (T&TEC's most recently reported SAIFI of 3.75 interruptions/customer and SAIDI of 308 minutes/customer). Dedicated, additional generation is not a preference — it is the only workable path.

2a.2 The Existing, Already-Proven Mechanism — a Fourth IPP-Style PPA

T&TEC does not generate its own power; it buys under long-term Power Purchase Agreements from independent power producers — PowerGen, TGU, and Trinity Power all supply under this exact structure. TGU itself was built as new, dedicated capacity when the country last needed a step-change in supply. The direct, already-proven mechanism for this load is a fourth IPP-style PPA, sized to the committed 450–800MW, following T&TEC's own existing commercial and legal precedent rather than inventing a new structure.

2a.3 Cost Comparison — Pure New-Build Versus Blended Repowering

Current market cost of new-build combined-cycle generation: BloombergNEF's most recent data (April 2026) puts new combined-cycle construction at US$2,157/kW, a 66% increase from under US$1,500/kW in 2023, driven by turbine, construction, and global pipeline cost inflation. Utility Dive independently confirms US$2.2–2.5 million per MW as the current build cost. Trinidad and Tobago's own direct precedent, TGU, cost approximately US$740 million for 720MW (2009–2013) — roughly US$1,028/kW, about half of today's price, meaning this build costs meaningfully more per megawatt than the country's last comparable project.

Repowering existing capacity — the materially cheaper alternative. Heat-recovery repowering (adding a gas turbine and Heat Recovery Steam Generator to an existing plant, reusing its steam turbine) is documented to increase net generating capacity by 150–200%, while reducing heat rate by 30–40% and cutting NOx emissions, at a real, documented cost of US$355–750/kW for the added capacity — a fraction of new-build cost. A real, executed precedent: TECO's Big Bend Station repowering added 200MW of capacity while cutting site CO2 emissions by over 2 million tons/year. This lines up directly with T&T-specific published literature: a carbon-pinch-analysis study of Trinidad and Tobago's own power sector found HRSG retrofits at existing plants could increase output by up to 38% without burning additional fuel — meaning this is not a generic global technique but one already identified as viable at T&T's own facilities.

6.4 Operator Training Time

An operator reaches production-quality output (the 200-clean-episodes-per-day benchmark) through a bounded process: one to two days of rig orientation and safety training, followed by three to five days of supervised practice runs with QA feedback, reaching independent production output by day five to seven — short because the task is bounded and repetitive by design, and the basis for facilities scaling operator headcount quickly once a first cohort is trained.

8.2 EDA Licensing — Real Market Data, Two Procurement Strategies

What real market data actually shows
Neither Synopsys nor Cadence publishes list prices; both price bespoke per customer. What is documented: node-locked commercial seats typically run US$50,000–500,000 per seat per year, with floating (concurrent-user) licenses at a 20–30% premium over node-locked; full front-to-back digital-implementation suites (the high end of that range and beyond) are not the relevant comparison here, since Sector 6 is scoped specifically to formal verification (Section 13.1), a narrower toolset than full RTL-to-GDSII design. Synopsys also offers Synopsys Cloud / FlexEDA, a pay-per-minute consumption model rather than annual seats — a materially better fit for a market entrant with uncertain, ramping utilisation than committing to fixed annual seats before Phase 1 demand is proven.

11Master Roadmap — Year 1 Through Year 3

Quarter 1 (FY2027 Q1)

RFP-16 (PMO) and RFP-17 (independent audit function) issued first. RFP-19, RFP-20 (legal advisory) and RFP-22 (NGC Gas Sales Agreement negotiation) issued in parallel — preconditions, not optional early work. Cabinet approval-in-principle and Attorney General legal review run concurrently.

Quarter 2 (FY2027 Q2)

RFP-1 through RFP-6 (TT Stack) issued as a batch. RFP-7 through RFP-13 (sector infrastructure) issued, sequenced by sector readiness — Sector 4 (RFP-10) and Sector 1 (RFP-7) go first, per the parliamentary document's own sequencing. RFP-25 (offtake advisory) begins active client origination.

Quarter 3 (FY2027 Q3)

RFP-14, RFP-15 (curriculum and certification) issued once Q2's sector infrastructure batch is far enough along to know what is actually being trained toward. RFP-23, RFP-24 (environmental review, co-ownership legal) complete. RFP-22's Gas Sales Agreement negotiation status is the single gating item for any further compute-utility progress.

Quarter 4 (FY2027 Q4)

First tranche-gate review (Section 25.3 of the parliamentary document) — no FY2028 capital call proceeds until this quarter's actual delivery is independently verified by RFP-17, not projected. RFP-27 (fund manager selection) should be far enough along that first Yozma fund formation is imminent.

Year 2 (FY2028)

Contingent entirely on Year 1's verified metrics. RFP-21 (fund administrator) active as first Yozma capital calls begin. Compute-utility Phase 2 capital releases only if RFP-25 has landed a signed offtake contract and RFP-22 a signed Gas Sales Agreement — the two hardest gates in the whole roadmap. RFP-18 (business development) shifts from setup to active enterprise-matching as Sector 7 certifications produce first graduating enterprises. RFP-26 (diaspora bond) launches if Year 1 registration work is complete.

Year 3 (FY2029)

Full sector scale-up per each sector's own Phase 3 targets. Second tranche-gate review determines whether the programme is tracking toward the wider or narrower end of the corrected TT$5.9–39.6 billion national mobilisation range — and this is not a failure state either way, it is the range the parliamentary document already plans for.
